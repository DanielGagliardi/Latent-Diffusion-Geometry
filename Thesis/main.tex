\section*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Preface}

Throughout my undergraduate and graduate studies in mathematics I have been caught between two main interests: the applied world of machine learning, and the theoretical world of topology and geometry. While this is often viewed as an unusual mix to choose, I have always found it greatly enjoyable.

This culminated in me wanting to answer the question of what is happening to the ``shape'' of data throughout a machine learning process, and also what even is the ``shape'' of data.

The value in understanding this is twofold: first, to be able to make better models by leveraging our understanding of how they work, and second, to be able to understand how to interpret what these models are doing. The dance between naturally arising data and the model trying to fit without knowing how the data is generated is a curious one. My main motivation was to try to understand this. 

One big idea is the notion of data lying on some low-dimensional space, which makes both learning and sampling from distributions supported on this lower-dimensional space easier. However this belief only truly simplifies the system in the small noise regime. All sorts of complex geometry can happen in the higher noise regimes. We do not, as we will see, have a nicely behaved interpolating family of manifolds.

Before doing the IASD M2 program, I had written a master's dissertation on diffusion models under the manifold hypothesis, under the supervision of George Deligiannidis. During this I became deeply interested in dimension estimation of datasets. This at best vauge and at worse terribly ill-posed question seemed to me one where and answer would tell us a lot about the natural geometry of data, and how diffusion models are able to make use of this. Almost every paper I found on the interplay between manifolds and diffusion models cited work from Eddie Aamari.

In November 2025 I reached out to Eddie Aamari to further discuss what the geometry of data could look like, and this led to this internship project. The initial goal was to understand the interplay between geometry affecting sampling error and how this could encourage simpler embeddings in a latent diffusion setting. However, given the complexity of work being done limited time was spent on investigating autoencoders so far.

A key challenge of this project has been stirking the balance between geometry: where things are well defined, and diffusion of probability densities: where simply by adding noise we violently change the notions of geometry we would like to equip our density with. This tradeoff has led to looking at several objects, which while interesting in their own right have not been helpful in bounding sampling error. We do think the final part on posterior covariance fields \autoref{sec:pcf} does provide new insight on where true complexity lies in the diffusion process.

\section{Anisotropic Stability Bound}
\label{sec:aniso_stab_bound}

We record a stability estimate for generator matching that keeps track of
\emph{which} directions of a drift error are amplified and which are damped.
The scalar one-sided Lipschitz estimate propagates every error with a single
worst-case rate; it is sharp only in an isotropic sense. The estimate below
replaces that scalar rate by the derivative flow of the learnt dynamics, a
matrix that sees the direction of the error. We isolate the mechanism in the
pure-transport case first: throughout Sections~\ref{subsec:mechanism}--%
\ref{subsec:scalar} the diffusion coefficient is set to zero, and the additive%
-noise case is recovered verbatim by averaging the derivative flow over the
Brownian trajectories (Remark~\ref{rem:diffusion}).

\subsection{The deterministic transport mechanism}
\label{subsec:mechanism}

Consider two time-dependent velocity fields $v_t,\hat v_t:\R^D\to\R^D$ for
$0\le t\le T$, smooth enough in space and integrable in time that their
characteristic flows are globally defined and differentiable in the initial
condition:
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac{\dd}{\dd s}\Phi_{t,s}(x)=v_s\big(\Phi_{t,s}(x)\big),
  \qquad
  \tfrac{\dd}{\dd s}\hat\Phi_{t,s}(x)=\hat v_s\big(\hat\Phi_{t,s}(x)\big),
  \qquad
  \Phi_{t,t}=\hat\Phi_{t,t}=\Id .
  \label{eq:flows}
\end{equation}
We write $\delta v_t:=v_t-\hat v_t$ for the velocity error and think of $v_t$ as
the exact field and $\hat v_t$ as the learnt one.

The estimate is built entirely from the derivative of the \emph{learnt} flow,
\begin{equation}
  \hat J_{t,T}(x):=D_x\hat\Phi_{t,T}(x),
  \qquad
  \tfrac{\dd}{\dd s}\hat J_{t,s}(x)=\nabla\hat v_s\big(\hat\Phi_{t,s}(x)\big)\,
  \hat J_{t,s}(x),
  \quad \hat J_{t,t}(x)=\Id .
  \label{eq:jacobian-ode}
\end{equation}
Thus $\hat J_{t,T}(x)h$ is the terminal displacement at time $T$ produced by an
infinitesimal perturbation $h$ applied at $(t,x)$ and transported by the learnt
dynamics. The learnt flow acts as a magnifying glass on errors: an error
committed at time $t$ is not scaled by a worst-case constant but carried by
$\hat J_{t,T}$ along its own direction. The following identity makes this exact.

\begin{lemma}[Telescoping identity along characteristics]
\label{lem:telescope}
For every initial point $x_0\in\R^D$, with $X_t:=\Phi_{0,t}(x_0)$ the exact
characteristic,
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{0,T}(x_0)-\hat\Phi_{0,T}(x_0)
  =\int_0^T \hat J_{t,T}(X_t)\,\delta v_t(X_t)\dd t .
  \label{eq:telescope}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Define $F(t):=\hat\Phi_{t,T}(X_t)$, which follows the exact dynamics up to time
$t$ and the learnt dynamics from $t$ to $T$. Its endpoints are the fully learnt
and fully exact terminal points, $F(0)=\hat\Phi_{0,T}(x_0)$ and
$F(T)=\Phi_{0,T}(x_0)$. Differentiating in $t$,
\[
  \tfrac{\dd}{\dd t}F(t)
  =\partial_t\hat\Phi_{t,T}(X_t)+D\hat\Phi_{t,T}(X_t)\,\dot X_t .
\]
Starting the learnt flow an instant later removes one short learnt step, so
$\partial_t\hat\Phi_{t,T}(x)=-D\hat\Phi_{t,T}(x)\,\hat v_t(x)$. Together with
$\dot X_t=v_t(X_t)$ and $D\hat\Phi_{t,T}(X_t)=\hat J_{t,T}(X_t)$ this gives
\[
  \tfrac{\dd}{\dd t}F(t)
  =\hat J_{t,T}(X_t)\big(v_t(X_t)-\hat v_t(X_t)\big)
  =\hat J_{t,T}(X_t)\,\delta v_t(X_t).
\]
Integrating $F(T)-F(0)$ from $0$ to $T$ yields \eqref{eq:telescope}.
\end{proof}

Identity~\eqref{eq:telescope} is the heart of the estimate. The error made at
each time enters the terminal gap through the derivative flow evaluated in the
actual direction of that error, with no worst-case constant and no loss.

\subsection{From the telescoping identity to the matrix bound}
\label{subsec:matrix}

We turn the pathwise identity into a bound on the terminal laws by coupling.
Let $X_0\sim\rho_0$ and transport it by both flows,
$X_t:=\Phi_{0,t}(X_0)$ and $\hat X_t:=\hat\Phi_{0,t}(X_0)$, with terminal laws
$\rho_T:=\mathrm{Law}(X_T)$ and $\hat\rho_T:=\mathrm{Law}(\hat X_T)$. Running both
flows from the same $X_0$ is a coupling of $\rho_T$ and $\hat\rho_T$, so
$W_1(\rho_T,\hat\rho_T)\le\E\|X_T-\hat X_T\|$. Applying
Lemma~\ref{lem:telescope} pathwise at $x_0=X_0$ and the triangle inequality,
\begin{equation}
  W_1(\rho_T,\hat\rho_T)
  \le\int_0^T\E\big\|\hat J_{t,T}(X_t)\,\delta v_t(X_t)\big\|\dd t
  =\int_0^T\!\!\int_{\R^D}\big\|\hat J_{t,T}(x)\,\delta v_t(x)\big\|
  \,\rho_t(\dd x)\dd t ,
  \label{eq:coupling-bound}
\end{equation}
where the last step uses $X_t\sim\rho_t$. It remains only to name the quadratic
form hidden in the norm.

\begin{theorem}[Anisotropic $W_1$ stability]
\label{thm:matrix}
Define the pullback amplification metric
\begin{equation}
  G_{t,T}(x):=\hat J_{t,T}(x)^{\!\top}\hat J_{t,T}(x),
  \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  \|\hat J_{t,T}(x)h\|^2=\big\langle h,\,G_{t,T}(x)\,h\big\rangle .
  \label{eq:pullback}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
  W_1(\rho_T,\hat\rho_T)
  \le\int_0^T\!\!\int_{\R^D}
  \big\langle \delta v_t(x),\,G_{t,T}(x)\,\delta v_t(x)\big\rangle^{1/2}
  \,\rho_t(\dd x)\dd t .
  \label{eq:matrix-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Substitute the identity in \eqref{eq:pullback} into \eqref{eq:coupling-bound}.
\end{proof}

The matrix $G_{t,T}(x)$ is the Euclidean metric at the terminal time pulled back
to the tangent space at $(t,x)$ by the learnt flow: it lives at time $t$ but
measures squared lengths after transport to $T$. Its eigenvectors are the input
directions at $(t,x)$ and its eigenvalues are the squared stretching factors of
those directions. In the deterministic case \eqref{eq:pullback} is an exact
identity, so no Cauchy--Schwarz slack is incurred in passing from the derivative
flow to the quadratic form. The bound \eqref{eq:matrix-bound} is anisotropic
precisely because it evaluates $G_{t,T}$ on the actual error direction
$\delta v_t(x)$ rather than on the worst possible direction.

\begin{remark}[Additive diffusion]
\label{rem:diffusion}
When both dynamics carry the same additive diffusion coefficient, the Brownian
term has no derivative in the initial condition, so the learnt derivative flow
$\hat J_{t,s}$ still solves the linear equation \eqref{eq:jacobian-ode} along
each trajectory. The metric becomes the trajectory average
$G_{t,T}(x)=\E\big[\hat J_{t,T}(x)^{\!\top}\hat J_{t,T}(x)\big]$, and Jensen's
inequality turns the exact identity in \eqref{eq:pullback} into the upper bound
$\E\|\hat J_{t,T}(x)\delta v_t(x)\|\le\langle\delta v_t,G_{t,T}\delta v_t
\rangle^{1/2}$, so \eqref{eq:matrix-bound} holds unchanged. The deterministic
case treated here is the load-bearing one.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Recovering the one-sided Lipschitz estimate}
\label{subsec:scalar}

The classical scalar estimate is the isotropic specialization of
Theorem~\ref{thm:matrix}, obtained by discarding the direction of the error.
Let $S_t(x):=\tfrac12\big(\nabla\hat v_t(x)+\nabla\hat v_t(x)^{\!\top}\big)$ be the
symmetric part of the learnt Jacobian, and suppose the learnt field is one-sided
Lipschitz with rate $\ell_t$, meaning
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_{\max}\big(S_t(x)\big)\le\ell_t
  \qquad\text{for all }x .
  \label{eq:osl}
\end{equation}
The condition constrains expansion only: it bounds the largest rate at which two
trajectories separate, and places no bound on contraction or on the rotational
part of $\nabla\hat v_t$.

\begin{corollary}[Scalar one-sided Lipschitz bound]
\label{cor:scalar}
Under \eqref{eq:osl},
\begin{equation}
  G_{t,T}(x)\ \preceq\ \exp\!\Big(2\!\int_t^T\ell_s\dd s\Big)\,\Id ,
  \label{eq:loewner}
\end{equation}
and consequently
\begin{equation}
  W_1(\rho_T,\hat\rho_T)
  \le\int_0^T\exp\!\Big(\int_t^T\ell_s\dd s\Big)\,
  \|\delta v_t\|_{L^1(\rho_t)}\dd t .
  \label{eq:scalar-bound}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Fix a direction $h$ and set $z_s:=\hat J_{t,s}(x)h$, which solves
$\dot z_s=\nabla\hat v_s(\hat\Phi_{t,s}(x))\,z_s$ by \eqref{eq:jacobian-ode}.
Only the symmetric part contributes to the growth of the norm, so
\[
  \tfrac{\dd}{\dd s}\|z_s\|^2
  =2\big\langle z_s,\,S_s(\hat\Phi_{t,s}(x))\,z_s\big\rangle
  \le 2\ell_s\|z_s\|^2 .
\]
Gr\"onwall's lemma gives
$\|\hat J_{t,T}(x)h\|\le\exp\!\big(\int_t^T\ell_s\dd s\big)\|h\|$, which is
\eqref{eq:loewner}. Inserting \eqref{eq:loewner} into \eqref{eq:matrix-bound}
and using $\langle\delta v_t,G_{t,T}\delta v_t\rangle^{1/2}\le
\exp(\int_t^T\ell_s\dd s)\|\delta v_t\|$ yields \eqref{eq:scalar-bound}.
\end{proof}

Corollary~\ref{cor:scalar} exhibits the scalar estimate as the Loewner
domination \eqref{eq:loewner}: the matrix $G_{t,T}$ is replaced by its largest
eigenvalue, uniformly over directions. The bound is correct, but it forgets the
eigenstructure of $G_{t,T}$, and in particular treats a strongly contracted
error direction as though it were the most expansive one. The next subsection
makes this gap concrete in a two-direction model, where a normal error is
carried by a contraction factor that the scalar constant cannot see.

\section{Posterior Covariance Field}
\label{sec:pcf}

Here we introduce the ultimate object we analyse: posterior covariance fields.

\begin{definition}
    Let $Y_t = X + \sqrt{t} Z$, $m_t(y) := \E\left[X \mid Y_t=y\right]$, and
    \[
    A_t(y) := \frac{1}{t} \Cov\left(X \mid Y_t=y\right).
    \]
    We call the weighted object $\mathfrak{F}_t := \Law\left(Y_t, m_t\left(Y_t\right), A_t\left(Y_t\right)\right)$ the \emph{posterior covariance field}. 
\end{definition}

Note: while we are also including the posterior mean $m_t(y)$ in our definition, we keep the name for simplicity. The posterior mean $m_t$ and by extension the score $s_t$ is the object whose regularity we want to understand, so its inclusion is natural.

There are a few reasons why it makes sense to look at this object. First, it is not plagued by the issues we encountered in the analysis of ridges or level sets in \autoref{sec:ridges} and \autoref{sec:level_sets}. These objects suffered from being too geometric for our use case, where we care about noise scales much greater than 0. We need a more analytic quantity, and the posterior covariance field is defined for all noise scales $t>0$. Second, the posterior covariance captures the local geometry of the score. The posterior covariance corresponds to the Jacobian of the score. This link is given in \autoref{sec:background}. Here we normalise the posterior covariance by $t$ to account for the scale of noise added. Third, here we deal with expectations, rather than $L^\infty$-type quantities like the reach $\tau_M$. If there exists a region of the density which is geometrically complex and as a consequence has poor regularity, but which carries almost no mass, we should not let it dramatically change our understanding of the behaviour of the score.

\subsection{Three functionals of the spectrum}

All three quantities below are moments of the same object: the spectral
measure of $A_t$, averaged over $y \sim p_t$. Write $a_1(y),\dots,a_D(y)$
for the eigenvalues of $A_t(y)$ and let
$\mu_t := \mathrm{Law}\bigl(a_I(Y_t)\bigr)$ with $I$ uniform on
$\{1,\dots,D\}$, so that $\E\sum_i f(a_i) = D\int f\,d\mu_t$ for any
$f$.

\subsubsection{Effective dimension}

\begin{definition}
\begin{equation}
\deff(t) \;:=\; \E\bigl[\tr A_t(Y_t)\bigr] \;=\; \E\sum_{i} a_i .
\label{eq:deff}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Exact MMSE identity]
\label{prop:deff-mmse}
Let $\mmse(t) := \E\bigl|X - m_t(Y_t)\bigr|^2$. Then
\begin{equation}
\deff(t) \;=\; \frac{\mmse(t)}{t}.
\label{eq:deff-mmse}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\tr\Cov(X\mid Y_t) = \E\bigl[|X-m_t(Y_t)|^2 \mid Y_t\bigr]$; take
expectations and divide by $t$.
\end{proof}

Identity \eqref{eq:deff-mmse} is exact, not asymptotic, and has two
consequences of practical importance. First, $\deff$ is dimensionless: it
is residual posterior uncertainty measured in units of injected noise
variance. Second, $\mmse(t)$ is precisely the population value of the
$\varepsilon$-prediction (or $x_0$-prediction) denoising loss at noise
level $t$, so $\deff$ requires no computation beyond the training
objective already being evaluated.

\paragraph{Link to dimension.}
An orthogonal projector of rank $k$ has spectrum
$\{1^{(k)},0^{(D-k)}\}$ and trace $k$. In the ideal manifold limit
$A_t\approx P_{T_x\M}$ and hence $\deff(t)\approx d$. Equivalently, via
\eqref{eq:deff-mmse}: a $d$-dimensional manifold leaves $O(t)$ posterior
uncertainty in each of $d$ tangent directions and $o(t)$ in the normal
directions, so $\mmse \approx d\times t$. Each eigenvalue may be read as a
fractional dimension: $a_i = 0.4$ contributes $0.4$ effective directions.

\paragraph{Failure mode.}
$\deff$ is a first moment and therefore cannot distinguish
$\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,0,0,0)$ from $\mathrm{diag}(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$:
both have trace $3$. It also over-counts when $a_i > 1$, in which case
the ``fractional dimension'' reading breaks down entirely. The next two
functionals repair exactly these two deficiencies.

\subsubsection{Distance to projector structure}

\begin{definition}
\begin{equation}
\dproj(t) \;:=\; \E\sum_i \min\bigl\{a_i^2,\;(a_i-1)^2\bigr\}.
\label{eq:dproj}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The summand is the squared distance from $a_i$ to the set $\{0,1\}$, and
the two branches exchange at $a_i = \tfrac12$:
\begin{equation}
\min\{a^2,(a-1)^2\} =
\begin{cases}
a^2, & a \le \tfrac12 \quad\text{(read as normal-like)},\\
(a-1)^2, & a \ge \tfrac12 \quad\text{(read as tangent-like)}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:dproj-frob}
Let $\Pset = \{P \in \R^{D\times D}: P = P^\top = P^2\}$ be the set of
orthogonal projectors and let $A$ be symmetric with eigenvalues $a_i$.
Then
\begin{equation}
\min_{P\in\Pset}\Frob{A-P}^2 \;=\; \sum_i \min\{a_i^2,(a_i-1)^2\},
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\dproj(t) = \E\bigl[\dist_F^2\bigl(A_t(Y_t),\Pset\bigr)\bigr].
\label{eq:dproj-frob}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Pset$ is invariant under conjugation by orthogonal matrices, and for
symmetric $A$ the Frobenius distance to a conjugation-invariant set is
minimized by a $P$ sharing the eigenvectors of $A$ (a consequence of the
Hoffman--Wielandt inequality). It then remains to round each $a_i$ to the
nearer of $0$ or $1$.
\end{proof}

Thus $\dproj$ answers: \emph{how far, on average, is the soft posterior
geometry from being a genuine tangent-space projector?} It vanishes
exactly when $A_t(Y_t)$ is a projector almost surely.

\begin{remark}[Interpretation as a resolution probe]
For data concentrated near a manifold but with normal thickness $\delta$,
the normal eigenvalues behave as $a_n \approx \delta^2/(\delta^2+t)$,
which passes through $\tfrac12$ at $t \approx \delta^2$ and contributes
$\tfrac14$ to $\dproj$ there. Consequently $\dproj(t)$ peaks at the scale
at which the manifold hypothesis stops being exactly true, and its peak
location is a readout of that scale. $\dproj$ is best regarded as a
scale-resolved test of manifold-likeness rather than a single verdict.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Ambiguity}

\begin{definition}
With $(x)_+ := \max\{x,0\}$,
\begin{equation}
\amb(t) \;:=\; \E\sum_i \bigl(a_i - 1\bigr)_+^2 .
\label{eq:amb}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The motivation is dynamical rather than geometric. $a_i > 1$ occurs if and only if the reverse probability-flow ODE is locally expansive along $v_i$. The threshold is the separatrix at which posterior variance exceeds the injected noise variance:
\begin{equation}
a_i = \frac{\text{posterior variance along } v_i}{\text{noise variance } t}
\quad\begin{cases}
<1: & \text{denoising, contraction},\\
=1: & \text{neutral},\\
>1: & \text{amplification, expansion}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The three design choices are then: threshold at $1$ (only expansion is
penalized), measure the \emph{excess} $a_i-1$ (the neutral point is $1$,
not $0$), and square it (making the functional comparable to a squared
Frobenius norm and penalizing strong expansion superlinearly).

\paragraph{Link to ambiguity.}
Suppose the posterior at $y$ is approximately
$\tfrac12\N(\mu_1,\Sigma) + \tfrac12\N(\mu_2,\Sigma)$, corresponding to
two competing clean configurations. Then
\begin{equation}
\Cov(X\mid Y_t=y) \;=\; \Sigma + \tfrac14(\mu_1-\mu_2)(\mu_1-\mu_2)^\top ,
\end{equation}
and the between-mode term can exceed $tI$ along $\mu_1-\mu_2$, giving
$a_i>1$. So $a_i > 1$ signals that the noisy observation fails to
identify a single locally stable latent configuration.

\begin{remark}[Scope of the interpretation]
Strictly, $a_i>1$ is a diagnostic that posterior variance exceeds the
noise scale. Multimodality is the typical, but not the only, mechanism:
heavy-tailed unimodal $p_0$ (e.g.\ Laplace, Student-$t$) also produces
$a_i>1$. ``Ambiguity'' is the geometric reading of the phenomenon, not a
theorem.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Mean versus tail]
$\amb$ is an average over $y\sim p_t$ and can be small while
$\sup_y a_{\max}(y)$ is large, if the expansive region carries little
mass. In practice one should report
both $\amb(t)$ and a high quantile of $(a_{\max}-1)_+$, since sampler
failure is driven by trajectories that enter the expansive region, not by
its measure.
\end{remark}

\subsection{MMSE-driven discretization}

We consider the additive Gaussian perturbation
\begin{equation}
    X_t = X_0 + \sqrt{t}\,Z,
    \qquad
    Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_D),
\end{equation}
so that, in the notation of the diffusion model,
\begin{equation}
    c_t = 1,
    \qquad
    \sigma_t^2=t.
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
    m_t(x) := \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t=x]
\end{equation}
denote the posterior mean and define the minimum mean-squared error
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MMSE}(t)
    :=
    \mathbb{E}\left[\|X_0-m_t(X_t)\|^2\right].
\end{equation}
Following the effective-dimension viewpoint, we write
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    :=
    \frac{\operatorname{MMSE}(t)}{t},
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MMSE}(t)
    =
    t\,d_{\mathrm{eff}}(t).
    \label{eq:mmse-deff}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Discretization error.}
For additive Gaussian noise, Tweedie's formula gives
\begin{equation}
    s_t(x)
    =
    \frac{m_t(x)-x}{t}.
\end{equation}
The same martingale argument used to control the score variation in the discretized reverse process then yields, for two noise levels
$t<t'$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    &
    \mathbb{E}
    \left\|
        s_t(X_t\mid t',X_{t'})
        -
        s_t(X_t)
    \right\|^2
    \\
    &\qquad =
    \frac{1}{t^2}
    \left(
        \operatorname{MMSE}(t')
        -
        \operatorname{MMSE}(t)
    \right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:score-mmse}
\end{equation}
with the sign understood according to the direction in which the diffusion trajectory is traversed. Thus, for a sufficiently fine discretization with step size
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t_k := t_{k+1}-t_k,
\end{equation}
the contribution of the $k$-th interval is, up to higher-order terms,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_k
    \;\asymp\;
    \frac{\left|\operatorname{MMSE}'(t_k)\right|}
         {t_k^2}
    (\Delta t_k)^2.
    \label{eq:local-error}
\end{equation}
Consequently, the total discretization error is approximated by
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{disc}}
    \;\asymp\;
    \sum_{k=0}^{K-1}
    a(t_k)(\Delta t_k)^2,
    \qquad
    a(t)
    :=
    \frac{|\operatorname{MMSE}'(t)|}{t^2}.
    \label{eq:discrete-objective}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Optimal allocation of time steps.}
We now choose the discretization points to minimize
\eqref{eq:discrete-objective} subject to a fixed number $K$ of steps.
Introduce the local step density
\begin{equation}
    n(t) := \frac{1}{\Delta t(t)}.
\end{equation}
In the continuum limit,
\begin{equation}
    K
    \approx
    \int n(t)\,dt,
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{disc}}
    &\approx
    \int a(t)\Delta t(t)\,dt
    \\
    &=
    \int \frac{a(t)}{n(t)}\,dt.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Minimizing this functional subject to
$\int n(t)\,dt=K$ gives
\begin{equation}
    n(t)\propto \sqrt{a(t)},
\end{equation}
and therefore
\begin{equation}
    \Delta t(t)
    \propto
    \frac{1}{\sqrt{a(t)}}
    =
    \frac{t}{\sqrt{|\operatorname{MMSE}'(t)|}}.
    \label{eq:mmse-schedule}
\end{equation}

Finally, using \eqref{eq:mmse-deff},
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{MMSE}'(t)
    =
    \frac{d}{dt}\left[t\,d_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)\right]
    =
    d_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    +
    t\,d_{\mathrm{eff}}'(t),
\end{equation}
so the resulting MMSE-adaptive discretization is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \Delta t(t)
    \propto
    \frac{t}
    {\sqrt{
        \left|
        d_{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
        +
        t\,d_{\mathrm{eff}}'(t)
        \right|
    }}
    }.
    \label{eq:natural-discretization}
\end{equation}

This prescription allocates more discretization points to regions in which the posterior uncertainty, measured by the MMSE, changes rapidly, while accounting for the $t^{-2}$ amplification of score errors at small noise scales.
